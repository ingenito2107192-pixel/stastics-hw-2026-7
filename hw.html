Two homework labs

Key-to-Address Lab

Lab 1 computes a toy public key Q = kG with the double-and-add algorithm, one step at a time. Lab 2 follows a mnemonic phrase to a checksummed Ethereum address.

Setup

Curve y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17), base point G = (1, 5)

G is on the curve because 5² = 25 ≡ 8 and 1³ + 7 = 8. The private key is a number k, and the public key is Q = kG. Double-and-add computes it by reading k in binary from left to right. The leading 1 sets R = G. After that, every bit doubles R, and a 1-bit also adds G.

λ = (3x₁²) · (2y₁)⁻¹ mod 17   (doubling: slope of the tangent)
λ = (y₂ − y₁) · (x₂ − x₁)⁻¹ mod 17   (adding: slope of the line)
x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂ mod 17   (third intersection, from the sum of the cubic's roots)
y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁ mod 17   (that point mirrored: y → −y)

    Statistical properties

    What each stage looks like statistically

    • Entropy: 128 uniform bits. The security of everything after this step rests on these bits.
    • Words: each word is one of 2048. In a 12-word phrase the last word holds 4 checksum bits, so a random wrong word is caught 15 times in 16.
    • Seed and child keys: they look uniformly random, but they are fully determined by the phrase.
    • Public key: its coordinates look random, but the point is algebraically tied to k, as Lab 1 shows.
    • Keccak-256: about 50% of output bits change when one input bit changes.
    • EIP-55: each letter a–f carries one check bit, about 15 per address. A mistyped address slips through roughly 0.0247% of the time (figure from the EIP).
    SHA-2 vs Keccak / SHA-3

    Same family, different designs

    SHA-2 (2001) chains a compression function (Merkle–Damgård). Keccak won the SHA-3 competition in 2012 and uses a "sponge" instead. NIST's final SHA-3 standard (2015) changed the padding byte from 0x01 to 0x06. Ethereum had already adopted the original Keccak, so Keccak-256 ≠ SHA3-256:

    Timeline and purpose

    Why several tools instead of one

    YearTechnologyJob in the wallet
    1977RSAFirst practical public-key system (not used by Ethereum, but the idea behind it)
    2001SHA-2 / SHA-512Hashing inside BIP-39 (checksum, PBKDF2) and BIP-32 (HMAC)
    2012BIP-32One seed → a tree of keys
    2013BIP-39Human-readable backup words with a checksum
    2014BIP-44Standard path m/44'/60'/0'/0/i so wallets agree
    2015SHA-3 (Keccak)Ethereum launched with Keccak-256 for addresses
    2016EIP-55Typo detection through letter case

    Each step solves a different problem. BIP-39/32/44 handle backup and organisation of keys. secp256k1 proves ownership through signatures without revealing the key. Keccak-256 turns a long, structured public key into a short identifier that looks random. EIP-55 catches typing mistakes. A hash cannot sign, a signature key makes a poor identifier, and neither is meant to be written on paper, so each job gets its own tool.
